Load Transfer Calculator

Braking, accelerating and cornering move load from one set of tires to another. Enter your car's weight, balance and center of gravity height to see how much moves, where it goes, and how the load on each tire changes when you brake into a corner.

Example values: a second-generation Firebird, ready to drive, with an LS swap. They are illustrative, not an ESS measurement. Weight and front % come from corner scales; the Balance & CG Builder estimates CG height.

What the car is doing

Filled in from the grip experiment on the home page: weight, front %, CG height, braking and cornering. The suspension values below are the same defaults it uses.

How it works

Front to rear. Braking pushes load onto the front tires; accelerating pushes it onto the rears. The amount is weight × CG height × g ÷ wheelbase. Only three things matter: weight, how high the mass sits, and how long the car is. Where the weight sits front to rear (the front %) changes the starting point, not the amount that moves.

Side to side. Cornering moves load to the outside tires: weight × CG height × g ÷ track, in total. A lower CG or a wider track moves less. How that total splits between the front and rear axles is set by the suspension: part travels straight through the suspension links (the roll centers), and the rest through the springs and anti-roll bars in proportion to each end's share of roll stiffness.

Why it matters. A tire's grip grows more slowly than the load on it, so the axle that carries more of the cornering transfer loses grip first. Shift the transfer toward the front and the car tends to push (understeer); toward the rear and it tends to rotate (oversteer). This is why a stiffer front anti-roll bar adds understeer.

What this tool assumes. Steady braking and cornering on a flat road; the whole car's mass is treated as one body (wheels and axles are not separated out); no aerodynamic downforce or drag; the front/rear split of cornering transfer uses the static axle loads. Tire grip is not modeled: the tool shows the loads, not whether the tires can hold them. These simplifications are fine for comparing changes on the same car; treat the absolute numbers as estimates.
