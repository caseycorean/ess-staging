Why Two Honest 0–60 MPH Times Disagree

Two people can time the very same launch and report different 0–60 mph results. One clock starts when the car first moves. The other starts after it has traveled a foot. Both stop at 60 mph. The second clock leaves out the first foot of the run.

Check the start and finish before you compare the times. The omitted time is called rollout. A car that covers the first foot quickly has less time to leave out, so adding a fixed number to every rollout time can give the wrong answer.

One launch, two clocks

Take a modeled 1990 Mazda MX-5 Miata with the 1.6-liter engine and five-speed manual. On the same run, ESS reaches 60 mph in 9.11 s from first motion. The model reaches one foot at about 0.37 s. Start the clock there and the same 60 mph arrival reads 8.74 s. The difference comes entirely from where the timer starts.

Why the offset varies

In the same ESS model, the 2024 Miata Club manual takes 6.80 s from first motion and 6.48 s with one-foot rollout. Its modeled 0.32 s offset is smaller than the 1990 Miata’s 0.37 s because it covers the first foot more quickly. Both examples are stock references modeled in ESS. See the 2024 reference

Read the test note before using the headline

Car and Driver gives a useful example of a method changing over time. In December 2019 it moved to a measured one-foot rollout. Its earlier method used a 3 mph start as an approximation. The current test guide says it publishes the rollout time so readers can add it back when comparing 0–60 mph from rest. Check the procedure for the particular test you’re reading. Car and Driver’s 2019 method change · Current test procedures

A drag-strip timeslip is a different kind of record. Its elapsed time starts as the car leaves the staging beam and ends at a distance marker. Actual rollout depends on how the car is staged and the beam geometry. The reaction time and the 60-foot split printed on a slip answer separate questions from a GPS 0–60 mph reading. NHRA starting-line and timing guide · Portatree on rollout and timing beams

Compare your runs on the same basis

  1. Match the start. Record first motion, a distance rollout, a speed threshold or the track’s timing method.
  2. Match the finish. 60 mph, 100 km/h and a quarter mile are different endpoints. Keep the unit with the number.
  3. Match the conditions. For a before-and-after comparison, keep the tires, surface, load and launch procedure comparable. Record differences in weather or correction methods. Conduct physical acceleration testing at a drag strip or other controlled facility.

See the clocks in ESS

Open the exact Miata in Replay and play the quarter mile. The animation clock starts at first motion. The result table reports about 16.71 s for the quarter mile with ESS’s one-foot rollout convention. That is a distance result. The 0–60 numbers above use a speed endpoint. On the reference page, the model estimate and the published test are labeled separately.
