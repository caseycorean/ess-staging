4 · Yaw inertia: how hard the car is to rotate

Yaw inertia change
–
lb·ft²

–

Yaw (or polar) inertia is the car's resistance to starting or stopping a rotation about a vertical axis — turning in, and catching a slide. Weight far from the center of gravity adds a lot of it; weight near the center adds little. That's why two cars with the same weight and the same 50/50 split can feel very different.

This is an estimate of the change, about each configuration's own center of gravity. Each listed part is treated as a single point weight, so a long assembly (a subframe, an exhaust) is approximated. It isn't the car's total yaw inertia — that needs a measured baseline — and it doesn't predict how the car will respond, which also depends on the tires, suspension and the turning force available. More: what 50/50 really means.

How it works

Each part's weight times its distance from the front axle is a moment. Removing a part takes its moment away; adding one puts a moment in; moving one does both; replacing one removes the old part and adds the new one. The new center of gravity is the total moment divided by the new total weight; front weight follows from where that center sits between the axles. The same arithmetic gives side-to-side balance and, when you supply a starting height, CG height.
