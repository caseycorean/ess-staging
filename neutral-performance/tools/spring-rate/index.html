Spring Rate & Ride Frequency Calculator

A spring's rate on the box is not what the wheel feels. Enter your springs, where they mount and what each corner weighs to see the rate at the wheel, how stiff the car rides (its ride frequency), and what spring you would need to reach a target.

The car

Example values: a second-generation Firebird with an LS swap and a live rear axle. They are illustrative, not an ESS measurement. Unsprung weight is what moves up and down with the wheel: the wheel, tire, brake and hub plus about half of the control arms, and for a live axle half of the whole axle. Tire rate: roughly 1,200–1,600 lb/in for a street tire; leave it blank to ignore the tire.

Front springs

Motion ratio = how far the spring compresses when the wheel moves 1 inch. For a coil spring on the lower control arm, measure from the arm's inner pivot to the spring's center and divide by the distance from the pivot to the ball joint. The example 0.55 is a typical value for this kind of front end (judgment, not measured).

Rear springs

Leaf springs, and coil springs or coilovers that sit on a live axle tube (as on a 3-link), move with the axle: use a motion ratio of 1. A leaf spring's published rate is already at the axle. The example 150 lb/in is illustrative.

How it works

Wheel rate. A spring mounted partway along a control arm moves less than the wheel does, and it also has less leverage over the wheel. Both effects multiply, so the rate at the wheel is the spring rate × motion ratio². A 500 lb/in spring at a 0.55 ratio is only about 150 lb/in at the wheel. A spring leaning over by an angle loses a little more: × cos(angle)². This is the most common mistake when people compare springs between different suspensions.

Ride rate. The tire is a spring too, sitting in series with the suspension. The ride rate the car body actually sits on is lower than either: wheel rate × tire rate ÷ (wheel rate + tire rate).

Ride frequency. How many times per second the body would bounce on its springs at that corner: 3.13 × √(ride rate ÷ sprung corner weight). It lets you compare stiffness between cars of different weights. Rough guide (published ranges, judgment): about 1.0–1.5 Hz for a comfortable road car, 1.5–2.0 Hz for a firm performance street car, 2 Hz and up for track cars without much downforce. Road cars usually run the rear slightly higher than the front so the car settles flat over a bump instead of pitching.

Spring for a target. Enter a target frequency and the tool works backwards through the tire and the motion ratio to the spring rate you would need.

What this tool assumes. Each corner is treated on its own (one wheel moving straight up and down); no anti-roll bars, bushings or damping; the motion ratio is constant through travel; the sprung weight is the corner weight minus the unsprung weight you entered, split equally left and right. Results are estimates for comparing setups, not a substitute for measuring the real installation.
